# Epilogue — The madness continues

The system I built to manage my anime collection is still part of my everyday life. I can follow new releases without constantly checking what has changed, and the repetitive work happens with little intervention.

By that measure, the automation worked.

There's a certain irony in all this: a system originally built to save time has given me plenty of opportunities to spend time improving it.

But that's part of the fun. Automation doesn't always end when the original problem is solved.

## Beyond anime

That approach did not stay confined to one project. I now build other personal tools, unrelated to anime, with the same goal: reducing friction in everyday life.

The development process has changed dramatically too. At first, I wrote the scripts myself. Later, a paid AI chatbot helped me explore unfamiliar technologies and architectural choices. Today, I can discuss a specification with the chatbot and delegate implementation to a coding agent.

One of my workflows is simple to describe:

**AI chatbot → remote connection → isolated coding-agent sandbox**

I decide what I want to achieve, discuss the approach, and let the agent experiment in a separate environment. I can give it considerable freedom there while keeping my everyday systems outside that experiment.

The responsibility to review the result, decide what is useful, and maintain the software remains mine. But the distance between an idea and a working prototype is much smaller.

## The gap with the workplace

One thing still discourages me: the pace of change in my personal projects feels very different from what I see at work.

Maybe that is partly a French thing. Or maybe companies simply have more constraints, more responsibilities, and good reasons to move carefully. I understand that, but the gap can still be frustrating.

Sometimes I wonder whether I am spending too much time exploring these tools. Will these ways of working become commonplace, or am I getting carried away with another technological experiment?

I do not know.

The tools and workflows will certainly evolve. But experimenting with them has already taught me a lot about defining problems, evaluating solutions, and deciding what is worth automating.

For now, that is reason enough to keep exploring.

## One last thing

I started with a few scripts that made computers do repetitive work for me.

Now I use AI assistants and coding agents to help me build the software that makes computers do repetitive work for me.

And you know what?

**I wrote this entire book with the help of a paid AI chatbot.**
